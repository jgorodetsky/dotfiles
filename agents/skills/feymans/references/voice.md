# The Feynman Voice — a forensic style analysis

This file exists because *how* Feynman wrote is inseparable from *what* made his
explanations work. The prose is not decoration on top of the pedagogy. It **is** the
pedagogy: the rhythm is what carries a reader who is right on the edge of getting it.

Everything below was derived by analysing a corpus of Feynman's own words. Where a
number appears, it was measured, not guessed.

---

## 0. The corpus

| Source | Year | Register | Words |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Six Easy Pieces* (Lectures on Physics I: ch. 1–4, 7, 37) | 1963 | teaching undergraduates | 50,638 |
| Nobel Lecture, *The Development of the Space-Time View of QED* | 1965 | technical, autobiographical | 11,558 |
| *There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom* | 1959 | technical talk to physicists | 7,144 |
| *What Is Science?* (NSTA address) | 1966 | lecture to schoolteachers | 6,599 |
| *Cargo Cult Science* (Caltech commencement) | 1974 | persuasive address | 3,976 |
| "O Americano, Outra Vez!" (Brazil), *Surely You're Joking* | 1985 | narrative memoir | 8,209 |
| **Total** | | | **≈88,000** |

Six registers, three decades, two media (written and transcribed speech). The traits
below are the ones that hold **across all of them** — those are the voice. Traits that
appear in only one register are noted as such.

A note on sourcing: the Lectures are free to read online but remain under Caltech
copyright — they are not public domain. This skill therefore encodes *derived rules*
and short attributed quotations for analysis and commentary. It does not redistribute
the texts. See [sources.md](sources.md).

## 1. Rhythm: the single most imitable trait

**Burstiness.** This is the signature, and it is measurable.

| Source | Mean | Median | ≤ 8 words | ≥ 35 words |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Six Easy Pieces* | 22.8 | 20 | 12.3% | 15.9% |
| Nobel Lecture | 28.0 | 25 | 4.8% | 25.1% |
| *Cargo Cult Science* | 20.6 | 17 | 16.2% | 15.1% |
| *What Is Science?* | 22.0 | 19 | 21.5% | 17.8% |
| *Plenty of Room* | 22.4 | 19 | 14.2% | 15.5% |

Read that as: a median around 20 words, with **roughly one sentence in eight under
eight words and one in six over thirty-five**. He does not write to an average. He
writes long, chained, comma-and-"and" sentences that accumulate a picture — and then
stops dead with something short.

The move, from the Nobel Lecture. A long technical build-up about charges and fields,
then:

> "Shake this one, that one shakes later. The sun atom shakes; my eye electron shakes
> eight minutes later, because of a direct interaction across."

Six words. Then a second short one that puts a human eye in the picture. That is the
whole rhetorical engine.

Another, from *Six Easy Pieces* on wave–particle duality:

> "So it really behaves like neither. Now we have given up. We say: 'It is like
> neither.'"

**To imitate:** after any passage of three or more long sentences, land a short one.
Never write three consecutive sentences of similar length.

## 2. Person: which pronoun, and when

Measured per 1,000 words:

| Source | I | we | you |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Six Easy Pieces* (teaching) | 1.78 | **19.95** | 2.74 |
| Nobel Lecture (narrating his own work) | **26.73** | 5.28 | 8.57 |
| *What Is Science?* (lecture) | **26.52** | 7.12 | 14.85 |
| *Cargo Cult Science* (persuading) | 16.85 | 6.54 | **20.62** |

The pattern is sharp and it is a rule you can apply:

- **"We" for working through the problem together.** Overwhelming in teaching mode —
  20 per 1,000 words, more than ten times his "I". "We shall now examine…", "Let us
  suppose…", "we find ourselves calculating more or less abstract things." You and the
  reader are on the same side of the desk, walking the same road, and neither of you
  has arrived yet.
- **"I" for confusion, error and story.** When he narrates his own wrong turns, "I"
  dominates. "I couldn't understand those very well." "I thought a few minutes, and I
  said, 'I'm sorry; I don't know.'"
- **"You" for instructions and for confrontation.** Highest in the commencement
  address, where he is telling people what to do. "You must not fool yourself."

**To imitate:** default to "we" while explaining. Switch to "I" the moment you are
admitting a limit or narrating a mistake. Use "you" for a direct instruction or a
direct challenge.

## 3. Questions

Question marks run at about **0.07 per sentence** across the corpus — roughly one
sentence in fourteen. In *Plenty of Room*, a talk built entirely around a provocation,
it reaches **13.3%**.

They are almost never rhetorical flourishes. He asks, then answers:

> "When I pull the wagon, the ball runs to the back, and when I am running with the
> wagon and stop, the ball runs to the front. Why?
>
> How would you answer?"

> "Why cannot we write the entire 24 volumes of the Encyclopaedia Brittanica on the
> head of a pin?
>
> Let's see what would be involved."

Note the second one especially: the question is *absurdly* concrete, and the answer is
immediate arithmetic. The question is a lever, not an ornament.

**To imitate:** pose the reader's own likely question in their words, then answer it
straight away. Do not stack unanswered questions.

## 4. The verbs are physical

Counts across the corpus: *jiggle* 27, *pull* 37, *push* 28, *shake* 20, *turn* 74,
*fall* 40, *hit* 14, *wiggle* 13, *stick* 8, *twist* 7, *rub* 7, *squeeze* 3,
*slosh* 3, *wobble* 1.

Water molecules **jiggle**. They do not "exhibit thermal agitation". The particles are
**"stuck together"**, **"glued together, so to speak"**, and if you **squeeze** two of
them they **repel**. Shoe leather wears out because it **rubs** against the sidewalk
and "the little notches and bumps on the sidewalk grab pieces and pull them off" — not
"because of friction", which he called *sad*, "because it's not science".

The rule underneath: **a verb the reader's body already knows costs zero to learn.** A
Latinate abstraction has to be decoded first.

**To imitate:** before writing an abstract verb, ask what the thing physically does.
Prefer grab, hold, drop, push, block, wait, copy, throw away.

## 5. Nouns come with sizes

He almost never leaves a thing unmeasured or unpicturable:

- A lump of sugar. A pair of pliers. In the dark.
- The head of a pin — "a sixteenth of an inch across".
- Twenty-eight blocks, weighing three ounces each, in a box weighing sixteen ounces.
- "If an apple is magnified to the size of the earth, then the atoms in the apple are
  approximately the size of the original apple."
- Old rectangular bathroom floor tiles from Long Island City.

That last one matters. It is not "some tiles". It is a specific, slightly odd,
unmistakably real object. **Specificity is a credibility device**: invented examples
are generic, remembered ones are strange.

**To imitate:** give quantities. Give brands, places, oddly specific objects. "A
sixteenth of an inch" beats "very small" every time.

## 6. Humour

Dry, deadpan, and pointed **at pomposity or at himself — never at the reader**.

- Pre-empting the impatient student: *"(So, not satisfied to learn physics in four
  years, you want to learn it in four minutes?)"*
- On his own teaching: *"I know that I don't know how to teach."*
- On his own lecture course: *"And if I did it again I wouldn't do it the same way — I
  hope I don't have to do it again!"*
- Predicting his own bad press after quoting a poem about a toad: *"I can see them all
  rushing out now to write some sort of headline which says: 'The Professor called the
  President of NSTA a toad.'"*
- Closing a graduation address with: *"Never say that you'll give a talk unless you
  know clearly what you're going to talk about and more or less what you're going to
  say."*

The jokes are short, land in one clause, and are never explained. Frequency is low —
about one exclamation mark per 36 sentences. He is not performing.

**To imitate:** at most one dry aside per section. Aim it at the difficulty, the
jargon, or yourself. Never at the reader's ignorance. Never explain the joke.

## 7. Reported dialogue as an explanatory device

Quotation marks run at **0.19 per sentence** — very high. He does not summarise
conversations; he stages them. And he uses the staging to *do the teaching*, because a
dialogue can carry a chain of reasoning that exposition would flatten:

> "It moves because the sun is shining."
> "No. What has that to do with the sun shining? It moved because I wound up the springs."
> "And why, my friend, are you able to move to wind up the spring?"
> "I eat."
> "What, my friend, do you eat?"
> "I eat plants."
> "And how do they grow?"
> "They grow because the sun is shining."

Eight lines, and a complete causal chain from a wind-up toy to stellar fusion. Try
writing that as a paragraph — it dies.

**To imitate:** when a concept is a *chain of causes*, or when the reader's likely
objection is a real one, write it as two voices. It is often shorter than exposition
and always more alive.

## 8. Scare quotes mark a word as a mere label

A recurring, deliberate tic. He puts quotes around a term precisely to signal *this is
just a name we agreed on, don't mistake it for understanding*:

- "the atomic hypothesis (or the atomic fact, or **whatever you wish to call it**)"
- "Now 10⁻⁸ cm is called an angstrom (**just as another name**)"
- "they are all 'particle waves,' **or whatever you want to call it**"
- Of a first-grade textbook's answer: "**that's only the definition of energy**; it
  should be reversed."
- "the correct statement… Experiment is the sole judge of scientific 'truth.'"

This is the bird lesson, applied at the level of punctuation.

**To imitate:** when you must introduce a term the reader could mistake for an
explanation, mark it as a label. "This arrangement is called a *mutex* — that's just
the name for it; the rule is what matters."

## 9. Epistemic honesty is constant, not occasional

Measured: *wrong/incorrect* 48 uses, *guess* 29, *we do not know* 17, *I don't know*
16, *approximate\** 19, *seems to* 19, *roughly* 12, *more or less* 11, *nobody knows* 4.

He builds ignorance into the explanation instead of hiding it at the end:

> "It is important to realize that in physics today, we have no knowledge of what
> energy is."

> "Even the experts do not understand it the way they would like to, and it is
> perfectly reasonable that they should not."

> "I understand it better now; but in my heart I still don't know where that circle
> is, where that pi comes from."

> "The facts are not correct; the spirit is correct."

That last line is remarkable — he tells you a story his father told him, then tells you
the story is factually wrong, then tells you why it was still worth telling.

**To imitate:** state limits in ordinary words, at the moment they become relevant, and
keep going. Never bury them in a hedge at the end.

## 10. Discourse markers he actually uses

Per 1,000 words: *that is* 1.47, *of course* 0.89, *for example* 0.77, *"So,"* 0.59,
*we shall* 0.57, *let us / let's* 0.55, *in fact* 0.49, *and so on* 0.48, *suppose*
0.45, *"Well,"* 0.44, *"Now,"* 0.43, *imagine* 0.31, *it turns out* 0.24, *you see*
0.18.

Two notes.

**"Now," and "Well," open a pivot**, the way a person actually speaks. "Now, what
should we teach first?" "Well, it seemed to me quite evident that…" They give the
reader a beat to catch up.

**"Of course" is frequent (0.89/1k) but never dismissive.** He uses it to acknowledge
shared ground or to deflate himself — "This is not necessarily the case of course",
"which of course are actually not attainable". He does not use it to mean *you should
already know this*. If your "of course" could make a reader feel behind, cut it.

## 11. What Feynman does *not* do — verified

Counted across the full ≈88,000-word corpus:

| Word | Occurrences | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| **basically** | **0** | 0.00/1k |
| trivial\* | 1 | 0.01/1k |
| "easy to see" | 1 | 0.01/1k |
| obviously | 5 | 0.06/1k |
| evident\* | 7 | 0.09/1k |
| clearly | 12 | 0.15/1k |

**Zero instances of "basically" in eighty-eight thousand words.** The most famous
explainer in modern physics never once reached for it. That is the empirical basis for
the banned-words rule in the main skill — it is not a stylistic preference.

He also does not:

- open at the technical level and climb down;
- use emoji, exclamation-heavy enthusiasm, or a cheerleading tone;
- flatter the reader;
- present a model without saying where it fails;
- pad. The prose is dense; the *ideas* are given room, the sentences are not.

## 12. The structural moves, named

These are the reusable devices, each traced to where he does it.

**The one-sentence compression.** *"If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge
were to be destroyed, and only one sentence passed on… what statement would contain the
most information in the fewest words?"* Force yourself to that sentence first; it
becomes the thesis line.

**The zoom ladder.** A drop of water at ×2000 ("as big as a large room"), ×2000 again
("fifteen miles across… like a crowd at a football game as seen from a very great
distance"), then ×250. A familiar handhold at every stop. Never jump orders of
magnitude bare.

**The escalating-complication analogy.** Dennis the Menace's 28 blocks: each new
complication — under the rug, out the window, Bruce's extra blocks, the locked toy box
weighed indirectly, the dirty bathwater — introduces exactly one new mechanism. Then:
*"there are no blocks."* The analogy is dismantled at the moment it has finished its job.

**The parallel experiment.** To explain electrons through two slits, first run the same
apparatus with bullets, then with water waves. The strangeness of the third is only
visible against the first two.

**Negative definition when no analogy is honest.** *"They do not behave like waves,
they do not behave like particles, they do not behave like clouds, or billiard balls,
or weights on springs, or like anything that you have ever seen."* Anaphora, and an
admission, doing the work an analogy cannot.

**Arithmetic in the open.** "Let's see what would be involved." Then the actual
division, with real numbers, in front of the reader.

**Declaring where the picture lies.** Immediately after presenting a diagram: the edges
are sharp and that is inaccurate; it is drawn in two dimensions for simplicity; the real
particles are jiggling and this is static; and this picture of steam has three molecules
where there would really be none.

**Naming the omission.** *"…so that you will have some feeling for what it is we are
leaving out."*

**Separating description from explanation.** *"We cannot explain the mystery in the
sense of 'explaining' how it works. We will tell you how it works."*

## 13. What not to carry over

Two things in the sources are of their time and are **not** part of the voice to imitate.

The 1966 NSTA address contains an extended passage of casual sexism about "the female
mind" and analytic geometry. The 1965 Nobel Lecture compares falling for a theory to
falling in love with a woman "only possible if you do not know much about her". Both
were unremarkable in their era and are indefensible now.

They are also, usefully, *separable*. Nothing in the explanatory machinery depends on
them. Take the rhythm, the concreteness, the honesty and the humour; leave the
period attitudes.

Likewise, do not imitate the persona. Feynman earned his asides by being Feynman.
Borrowed swagger reads as arrogance. **Copy the method and the rhythm, not the
celebrity.**

## 14. Carrying this voice into another language

Everything measured above is English. The numbers are properties of *English prose* and
they do not transfer: a 20-word median means nothing in Chinese, and pronoun rates mean
nothing in a pro-drop language like Portuguese or Spanish, where the subject is usually
omitted outright.

**What transfers is the shape, not the statistics.**

| Transfers | Does not transfer |
|---|---|
| Sharp variation in sentence length | The 20-word median |
| Physical verbs over abstract nouns | The specific verbs (*jiggle*, *shake*) |
| Concrete objects with real quantities | English measurement units |
| A question posed, then answered at once | The 7%-of-sentences rate |
| Ignorance admitted plainly, mid-flow | The English phrasings of it |
| Terms marked as mere labels | Scare-quote conventions (they differ) |
| Dry humour aimed at pomposity | The specific jokes |

### The failure this prevents

The disease is **calque**: composing in English and translating, so English syntax and
idiom survive into a language that would never phrase it that way. Every word is
correct and the paragraph still reads foreign. Real examples caught in this repository's
own Portuguese README, before a native speaker rejected it:

| Written (calque) | English behind it | What a native writes |
|---|---|---|
| "Você concorda com a cabeça." | "You nod." | "Você balança a cabeça." |
| "Nada foi transferido." | "Nothing was transferred." | "Nada foi transmitido." |
| "uma imagem que qualquer um consegue segurar" | "a picture anyone can hold" | "uma imagem que qualquer um consegue formar na cabeça" |
| "uma subida graduada" | "a graded climb" | "uma subida em degraus" |
| "Marchar um especialista por cinco níveis" | "marching an expert through five levels" | "Arrastar um especialista pelos cinco níveis" |
| "acha os seus buracos" | "finds your holes" | "encontra as suas lacunas" |
| "com toda simplificação rotulada como tal" | "every simplification labelled as one" | "e toda simplificação vem marcada como tal" |

Note the pattern: none of these are mistranslations. Each is a *correct* rendering of
the English words and a *wrong* rendering of the English intent. That is what makes the
failure hard to catch from inside — you have to read the paragraph as someone who has
never seen the source.

### The same failure, committed a second time

The rule above was already written down when this repository's header image was built —
and the header was still produced by writing English and translating it nine ways. A
native speaker rejected the Portuguese on sight:

| Written (calque) | English behind it | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| "às vezes você vê a página de ontem" | "you sometimes see yesterday's page" | Portuguese has no such expression. Nobody "sees yesterday's page". |
| "uma frase em que você balançou a cabeça" | "a sentence you nodded at" | Not Portuguese, and *balançar a cabeça* can read as **no**. |
| "Agora é seu" | "Now it's yours" | Fine English. Inert in Portuguese. |

**Knowing the rule does not enforce it.** What fixed it was changing the process, not
adding more warnings: each language was given the *specification* — what must be
conveyed, and to whom — and written from scratch by someone composing in that language.

The results are the argument. Asked for a label meaning "something you agreed to without
reading", the versions written natively landed on things translation could not reach:
Portuguese quoted the checkbox itself (**"LI E CONCORDO"**), Russian named the button
(**«ПРИНЯЛИ НЕ ЧИТАЯ»**), Chinese described the gesture (**"你顺手点了同意"** — *you
clicked agree offhandedly*). Each is idiomatic, and none is a translation of the others.

The lesson for this skill: when the answer must be in another language, **do not draft
in English first.** Take the specification and start there.

### Per-language notes

- **Portuguese / Spanish** — pro-drop: an explicit subject pronoun in every sentence is
  the loudest tell of translated English. Prefer verbs to nominalisations.
- **French** — non-breaking space before `: ; ! ?`, « » for quotations, and resist
  anglicisms where a real French technical term exists.
- **Chinese** — full-width punctuation; break long English-style sentences into the
  short clauses Chinese prefers; avoid stacked attributives before 的 and gratuitous 被.
- **Russian** — free word order carries the emphasis English carries with structure; « »
  for quotes; watch aspect.
- **Hindi / Bengali / Urdu** — SOV, not SVO. Developers routinely use English loanwords
  in the local script; that is usually better than an invented pure-native coinage.
- **Arabic / Urdu** — verbal sentences over the nominal calque of English; in Markdown,
  keep code fences outside any `dir="rtl"` block so commands stay copy-pastable.

### The check

Read it back as a monolingual native of that language who has never seen English. Would
they believe a compatriot wrote it? "It reads like a good translation" is a failure, not
a pass.

## 15. Calibration passage

If you want one paragraph to tune against, use this — the transition in *Six Easy
Pieces* from a picture of water to what heat actually is. Long chain, then a short
landing, physical verbs, a familiar object, a mechanism, and no jargon at all:

> "Now imagine this great drop of water with all of these jiggling particles stuck
> together and tagging along with each other. The water keeps its volume; it does not
> fall apart, because of the attraction of the molecules for each other. If the drop is
> on a slope, where it can move from one place to another, the water will flow, but it
> does not just disappear — things do not just fly apart — because of the molecular
> attraction. Now the jiggling motion is what we represent as heat: when we increase
> the temperature, we increase the motion."

Count what is happening there. Four sentences. The word "heat" arrives last, *after*
the reader has already watched the thing it names. Mechanism first. Name last.
