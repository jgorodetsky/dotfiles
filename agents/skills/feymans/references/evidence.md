# Evidence — what the research supports, and where this method fails

The Feynman Technique is popular, which means most of what is written about it is
uncritical. This file separates what has empirical support from what is folklore, and is
honest about the failure cases. Rule zero applies to the skill's own claims.

---

## 1. What holds up

### The illusion of explanatory depth
Rozenblit & Keil (2002), *Cognitive Science* 26(5). People rate their understanding of
how everyday things work far higher than is warranted; the rating collapses as soon as
they must produce a step-by-step mechanistic account, and they revise their self-rating
downward afterwards.

Crucially, the effect is **specific to explanatory knowledge** — causal, mechanistic
understanding. It is much weaker for facts, procedures and narratives.

**Why it matters here:** this is the mechanism the whole technique exploits. "Explain it
plainly" is not a communication exercise, it is a *diagnostic* that makes an invisible
gap visible. It is also why the self-audit in §7 of the main skill is not optional: the
writer is subject to the same illusion as the reader.

### The self-explanation effect
Chi et al. (1989), *Cognitive Science* 13(2). Students who spontaneously generate
explanations to themselves while studying worked examples learn substantially more than
those who don't — and the effect can be induced by prompting.

**Why it matters here:** generating an explanation beats receiving one. This is the
research basis for coach mode (§10). When someone wants to *learn* rather than to
*know*, having them explain is stronger than explaining at them.

### The protégé effect / expecting to teach
Nestojko et al. (2014), *Memory & Cognition* 42(7). Participants told they would teach
material later recalled it better and more coherently than those told they would be
tested — **even though the teaching never happened**. The framing alone produced the gain.

**Why it matters here:** telling a user "explain this back to me as if I'd never seen it"
does real work even if the explanation is never finished.

### The curse of knowledge / expert blind spot
Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989) named it in economics; Nathan & Petrosino (2003) and
Nathan & Koedinger documented it in teaching. As expertise deepens, mental
representations compress and become automatic, and experts systematically misjudge what
novices know — often predicting that novices find *abstract formalism* easier than
concrete stories, when the reverse is true.

**Why it matters here:** it is the justification for the vocabulary lock. "Everyone knows
what a hash is" is exactly the judgement the bias corrupts. The lock replaces a corrupted
judgement with a mechanical rule.

## 2. What is folklore

**"Feynman invented the Feynman Technique."** He did not. He never published a named
four-step method. The four-step formulation was assembled by later popularizers, mainly
Scott Young around 2011, as a fair reconstruction of how Feynman worked. Say "the method
named after Feynman", not "Feynman's method".

**"Explain it to a 12-year-old."** A useful heuristic, not something Feynman said. His
own operational test is sharper and is documented: *"Without using the new word which you
have just learned, try to rephrase what you have just learned in your own language"*
(*What Is Science?*, 1966). Prefer his version — it is checkable, where "would a
12-year-old get this?" is a guess.

**"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it" — Einstein.** Almost
certainly not Einstein; no primary source. The closest genuine Feynman anecdote is
David Goodstein's account: asked to prepare a freshman lecture on spin-½ particles,
Feynman came back and said he couldn't do it, and concluded *"that means we really don't
understand it."* Note the honesty — it's about the limits of physics, not a motivational
slogan. Attribute it as an anecdote reported by Goodstein, not as a Feynman quotation.

**"Any topic can be made simple."** No. See below.

## 3. Where the method genuinely fails

Being straight about this is required by rule zero.

### Expertise reversal
Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003) and a substantial follow-up literature: guidance
that helps novices becomes *actively harmful* to experts. Worked examples, elaborate
scaffolding and redundant explanation impose extra load once the learner already holds
the schema.

**Consequence for this skill:** the ladder is a service, not a ritual. Thesis first,
compact form when the reader is expert or the question is narrow. Marching a specialist
through a kitchen-table analogy is not thoroughness — it measurably degrades their
comprehension, and it reads as condescension.

### Irreducible complexity
Some things cannot be reduced without becoming false. Feynman himself hit this wall,
repeatedly and publicly:

> "Because atomic behavior is so unlike ordinary experience, it is very difficult to get
> used to and it appears peculiar and mysterious to everyone, both to the novice and to
> the experienced physicist. Even the experts do not understand it the way they would
> like to."

And his father asked him, after MIT and Princeton, how a photon can come out of an atom
without having been inside it. Feynman's answer:

> "I'm sorry; I don't know. I can't explain it to you."

**Consequence:** when reduction would falsify, say so, describe the behaviour without
claiming to explain the cause, and use negative definition. The honest floor for some
topics is *"here is what it does; nobody has a picture of why"*. That is a complete
answer, not a failed one.

### Procedural and physical skills
The technique targets conceptual, causal understanding. It does little for motor skills,
tacit craft knowledge, or memorised correspondences (vocabulary, keybindings, drug
dosages). Those need retrieval practice and spacing, not explanation. Don't apply the
ladder to a lookup table.

### Cost
Doing this properly is slower and longer than answering the question. For a quick factual
lookup, the full ceremony is waste. Compact form exists for this reason.

### The fluency trap
A well-built explanation *feels* like understanding to the person reading it. That feeling
is not evidence — it may just be the illusion of explanatory depth, restored. Where it
matters, the honest close is to push the reader toward generating it themselves rather
than admiring the explanation.

## 4. Practices this skill deliberately does not adopt

**Learning styles.** No credible support for matching instruction to "visual/auditory/
kinaesthetic" learners. The skill uses concrete physical imagery because it is
concrete — a property of the *material*, not of a learner type.

**"Dumbing down."** Not a goal and not a synonym for level 0. The concepts at level 0 are
different concepts, not degraded ones.

**Enthusiasm as a substitute for clarity.** Feynman's prose is warm and almost never
exclamatory — about one exclamation mark per thirty-six sentences, and no cheerleading.
Interest comes from the subject being genuinely interesting.
