# Sources and provenance

Consult this before attributing anything to Feynman. Popular writing about him is full
of quotations he never said, and getting a Feynman quote wrong while invoking his name
for intellectual honesty is a bad look.

### On copyright

None of this is public domain. The Feynman Lectures are freely *readable* at Caltech's
site, which is not the same as freely *redistributable*.

This skill therefore contains **derived stylistic rules, structural analysis, and short
attributed quotations** used for commentary and instruction. It does not bundle or
redistribute the source texts. If you fork this repository, keep it that way: quote
briefly, always attribute, never paste chapters.

## Verified quotations

Safe to use, with the attribution given.

**On names versus understanding** — *What Is Science?*, 1966:
> "You can know the name of that bird in all the languages of the world, but when you're
> finished, you'll know absolutely nothing whatever about the bird… There is a difference
> between the name of the thing and what goes on."

(The talk gives it as reported speech from his father, in a slightly longer form. The
short version above is the commonly quoted condensation; if you need it exact, quote the
final sentence, which is verbatim.)

**The rephrase test** — *What Is Science?*, 1966:
> "Without using the new word which you have just learned, try to rephrase what you have
> just learned in your own language."

**On the emptiness of definitions** — *Surely You're Joking*, Brazil chapter:
> "And there, have you got science? No! You have only told what a word means in terms of
> other words. You haven't told anything about nature."

**The triboluminescence rewrite** — same chapter:
> "When you take a lump of sugar and crush it with a pair of pliers in the dark, you can
> see a bluish flash. Some other crystals do that too. Nobody knows why. The phenomenon
> is called 'triboluminescence.'"

**Keyword-only knowledge** — same chapter:
> "If I asked, 'What is Brewster's Angle?' I'm going into the computer with the right
> keywords. But if I say, 'Look at the water,' nothing happens — they don't have anything
> under 'Look at the water'!"

**On self-deception** — *Cargo Cult Science*, 1974:
> "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person
> to fool."

**On explaining to non-experts** — same address:
> "I would like to add something that's not essential to the science, but something I kind
> of believe, which is that you should not fool the layman when you're talking as a
> scientist."

**On cargo cult form** — same address:
> "They're doing everything right. The form is perfect. It looks exactly the way it looked
> before. But it doesn't work. No airplanes land."

**The atomic hypothesis** — *Six Easy Pieces*, ch. 1:
> "If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge were to be destroyed, and only one
> sentence passed on to the next generations of creatures, what statement would contain
> the most information in the fewest words? I believe it is the atomic hypothesis…"

**On approximation** — same chapter:
> "Each piece, or part, of the whole of nature is always merely an approximation to the
> complete truth… Therefore, things must be learned only to be unlearned again or, more
> likely, to be corrected."

**On the limits of understanding** — *Six Easy Pieces*, ch. 6:
> "Even the experts do not understand it the way they would like to, and it is perfectly
> reasonable that they should not."
> "We cannot explain the mystery in the sense of 'explaining' how it works. We will tell
> you how it works."

**On energy** — *Six Easy Pieces*, ch. 4:
> "It is important to realize that in physics today, we have no knowledge of what energy
> is."
> "The most remarkable aspect that must be abstracted from this picture is that there are
> no blocks."

**On mystic formulas** — *What Is Science?*, 1966, on "energy makes it move":
> "It would be equally well to say that 'God makes it move,' or 'spirit makes it move,' or
> 'movability makes it move.'"

**On teaching design** — Feynman's Preface, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*:
> "I tried very hard to make all the statements as accurate as possible, to point out in
> every case where the equations and ideas fitted into the body of physics, and how — when
> they learned more — things would be modified."

**On expertise** — *What Is Science?*, 1966:
> "Science is the belief in the ignorance of experts."

**On honest reporting** — *Cargo Cult Science*, 1974:
> "If you're doing an experiment, you should report everything that you think might make
> it invalid — not only what you think is right about it."

## Do not attribute these to Feynman

**"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough."**
Usually credited to Einstein. No primary source for either man. Do not use it as a
quotation from anybody.

**"I don't know how to teach a freshman course on spin-½ particles… that means we really
don't understand it."**
This is a real anecdote but it is **reported by David Goodstein**, not written by
Feynman. Attribute it as "an anecdote related by David Goodstein" rather than as a
Feynman quotation. The wording varies between retellings.

**The four steps of "the Feynman Technique."**
Not his formulation. Assembled by later popularizers, chiefly Scott H. Young (c. 2011).
Say "the technique named after Feynman".

**"Notebook of Things I Don't Know About."**
This one is real, from James Gleick's biography *Genius* (1992) — but it is Gleick's
account of Feynman's graduate-school habit, not a method Feynman taught. Don't inflate
it into a study system he endorsed.

**"Study hard what interests you the most in the most undisciplined, irreverent and
original manner possible."**
Genuine — from a 1966 letter, collected in *Perfectly Reasonable Deviations from the
Beaten Track*. Fine to use, but check the collection rather than a quote site.

## The parts of the sources not to imitate

Noted here so nobody is surprised on reading the originals.

The 1966 NSTA address contains an extended passage of casual sexism about "the female
mind" and analytic geometry. The 1965 Nobel Lecture likens falling for a theory to
falling in love with a woman "only possible if you do not know much about her". Feynman's
conduct toward women is a documented and legitimate part of how he is assessed today.

None of the explanatory machinery depends on any of it. Take the method; leave the
period attitudes. See [voice.md §13](voice.md).
